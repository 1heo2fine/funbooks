Biology: Life Sciences Chapter 7 — Cellular Energy
Chapter 7

Cellular Energy: Photosynthesis & Respiration

7.1 Overview of Energy in Living Systems

All living organisms require a continuous supply of energy to carry out the fundamental processes of life — growth, reproduction, movement, and homeostasis. In biological systems, this energy is stored and transferred primarily through the molecule adenosine triphosphate (ATP). ATP acts as the universal energy currency of the cell, coupling energy-releasing reactions to energy-requiring ones.

Energy flows through ecosystems in one direction — from sunlight to producers and then through consumers — while matter cycles continuously. The two central processes that govern this flow are photosynthesis, which captures light energy, and cellular respiration, which releases stored chemical energy.

7.2 Photosynthesis: Capturing Light Energy

Photosynthesis occurs primarily in the chloroplasts of plant cells and in the cells of algae and cyanobacteria. The overall equation can be summarized as:

Equation 7.1 — Overall Photosynthesis
6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

This deceptively simple equation conceals two distinct sets of reactions occurring inside the chloroplast. The light-dependent reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes and capture sunlight, producing ATP and NADPH while releasing oxygen as a by-product. The Calvin cycle (light-independent reactions) occurs in the stroma and uses the ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into organic molecules.

7.2a The Light-Dependent Reactions

When light strikes a chlorophyll molecule embedded in Photosystem II, electrons become energized and leave the reaction center. These energized electrons travel down the electron transport chain, releasing energy that pumps H⁺ ions across the thylakoid membrane. The resulting electrochemical gradient drives ATP synthase to phosphorylate ADP, producing ATP — a process called photophosphorylation.

Meanwhile, water molecules are split in a process called photolysis, replenishing the electrons lost by chlorophyll and releasing O₂ gas. At Photosystem I, electrons are re-energized and ultimately reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH, which carries high-energy electrons to the Calvin cycle.

Figure 7.2 — Summary of Light-Dependent Reactions
Photosystem II → Electron Transport Chain → ATP Synthase → Photosystem I → NADPH
Water (H₂O) is split at PSII; oxygen released as waste product.

7.2b The Calvin Cycle

The Calvin cycle uses the ATP and NADPH produced during the light-dependent reactions to convert CO₂ into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P), a three-carbon sugar that serves as a precursor for glucose and other organic molecules. The enzyme RuBisCO catalyzes the critical step of carbon fixation, attaching CO₂ to the five-carbon acceptor molecule ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP).

The cycle must turn three times (fixing three CO₂ molecules) to produce one net molecule of G3P, consuming 9 ATP and 6 NADPH in the process. Six turns of the cycle are needed to produce one glucose molecule.

7.3 Cellular Respiration

Cellular respiration is the process by which cells break down organic molecules — primarily glucose — to release the energy stored in their chemical bonds. This energy is captured as ATP and used to power cellular work. The overall equation is essentially the reverse of photosynthesis:

Equation 7.2 — Overall Cellular Respiration
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ATP (energy)

Cellular respiration occurs in three major stages: glycolysis (in the cytoplasm), the Krebs cycle (in the mitochondrial matrix), and oxidative phosphorylation (at the inner mitochondrial membrane). Aerobic respiration produces approximately 36–38 ATP molecules per glucose molecule, making it far more efficient than anaerobic pathways.
